## How to read the table

**Verdicts.** They mean the cost of our next unit of attention, not our
enthusiasm.

| verdict | meaning |
|---|---|
| **adopt** | we use it, or we copy its procedure into what we already run |
| **evaluate** | the artefact exists and is cheap to test; a bounded test is worth a day |
| **watch** | keep it on the list; something about it will change, and the trigger is named |
| **skip** | set it down deliberately, with the reason recorded so nobody re-reads it |

**"Fits" is three separate questions**, and a row can pass the first and fail
the others:

1. **capacity** — does the artefact fit the box's memory?
2. **policy** — may it run there? Our rule (below) is stricter than capacity.
3. **work-fit** — would it be fast enough for the job we would give it? The
   Thor is bandwidth-bound at small batch, so "it loads" is not "it answers".

**The rules in force**, quoted from `projects/builder-ide/CLAUDE.md`:

> **No LLMs on the Thor** (Casey, B2, 2026-10-31). Language comes from cloud
> subscriptions (z.ai, MiniMax, Claude, others), each a parameter priced per
> call. The Thor is for motion as virtual robotics, visual assets, world models
> and engines, and many small decision models.

> **Any other third-party project from GitHub still needs his OK.**

> **Move fast; permissive by default** … No transcripts or audio are stored.

> **Licences are a checked list**, especially for models: some image models are
> non-commercial.

Two precedents decide most of the local-model rows below: **Seed-OSS-36B-Instruct**
(Apache-2.0) is our one scoped exception to B2 — our own NVFP4 conversion via
thor-fp4, served under a thor-admit lease, never resident; and **SceneBloom**
runs with "its VLM swapped to a cloud vision model we already use … or locally
as a scoped B2 exception". So a local model is reachable, but it has to be
earned as an exception, and it must arrive as a number.

**The two boxes.** Thor: Jetson AGX Thor, 122 GB unified memory (CPU and GPU
share it), Blackwell sm_110, CUDA 13.2+, NVFP4 tensor cores; thor-fp4 measures
0.5625 B/parameter and 371–456 TFLOPS on the cuBLASLt NVFP4 path at large K.
ProArt: RTX 4050, 6 GB VRAM (6141 MB), Ada sm_89 — and its VRAM is already
committed: the first mile's voice rungs 3 and 4 sit at **5426 MB of 6141 MB**,
leaving about **715 MB** free.

**Notation.** "GB" is decimal and "GiB" binary, as the sources state them.

## 2. What to do next, ranked by value to us

Each item is an experiment with a measured result or a measured no, not an
opinion. Numbers go to `docs/lab/` with the script beside them, per our rules.

**1. Host Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe on the Thor and measure it against the first
mile's current ears.**
Why it is first: it is the only item in the roundup whose vendor names our
silicon (Jetson Thor, sm_110, BF16/FP8/NVFP4) and ships an NVFP4 build at
21.56 GB that fits 122 GB unified memory. The hardware fact: NVIDIA's own
compatibility table lists it; the old way failed because our ears are
LFM3-Audio-2B at 49–56 ms on a 6 GB laptop, with a Groq whisper path behind it.
What changes: the Thor holds a 40-language ASR model with diarisation and word
timestamps. What it makes possible: a quality floor behind the live path — the
live path still answers in tens of milliseconds, the Thor model checks.
Measure: WER and latency on our own synthetic-reference audio (never a human
recording, and nothing stored), against the current path; the box state beside
the numbers. Do it under a thor-admit lease, never resident — 122 GB is
residency-limited, not capacity-limited. **Needs his OK** (new third-party
project).

**2. Run SLEEP's dreaming procedure inside one night.**
Why: our night consolidates artefacts, but it has no rehearsal step with a
selection criterion — the gap dsmc names between "consolidation" and
"learning". SLEEP's load-bearing part is not the weight update (which needs
LoRA-scale training and a MoE architecture we do not have) but the dreaming
stage: generate candidate behaviours, score them, rehearse only the top. The
paper's own ablation makes it load-bearing — removing dreaming collapses
SQuAD from 48.1/44.3 to 35.7/36.2. Measure: one night with and one without the
rehearsal step, then the judge's ledger: does the next night's decision quality
move? Cost: no new model, no new hardware — the existing seats already write
the candidates.

**3. Measure our judge against the bar Clover published.**
Why: the judge already answers linked questions from a shared state, and
Clover's decision model reports exactly that task: 2420 linked questions in 500
groups, 95.0% of questions correct and 80.4% of groups fully correct in joint
mode, against TypeSafe Jev's 63.6% and 15.8% on the same questions. We have
never measured our own judge against a published number, and the weights are not
released, so the bar is the usable part. Measure: build 50 linked groups from
our own judge contexts, score d1-2B (τ = 0.5), Jev and Clef in independent and
joint mode; record the distribution, cost and latency in the ledger the way
every judgment is already recorded. If our joint mode is already near 95%,
that is a finding; if it is near Jev's 63.6%, we have found our next project.

**4. Put AutoSwarm's acceptance conditions on our delegation seam.**
Why: AutoSwarm's contribution for us is a data structure, not a tool —
"the intended use of a result is part of its delegation" — which is the shape
of our gaps → precedent store → BoardManifest plan, and the shape of the
`judge@<backend>:<model>` records we already write. Measure: an A/B over one
night — contributions carry acceptance conditions and dependencies, and we count
how many the judge later admits that it should not have. No hardware, no
installs beyond a clone into a worktree, and his OK for the dependency.

**5. Re-price our cloud seats, with the volatility the S-1 admits.**
Why: the judge's top rung is literally "a cloud LLM work order", and we have
four candidates we have not compared on our own traffic: GPT-6.1 Terra
($1/$5, effort none→max, 1.05M context), Claude Fable 5.1 (≈25% cheaper per
typical workload, up to 45% on complex coding and agentic tasks, per the
filing), Claude Opus 5.5 (≈40% cheaper per task), and Gemini 4 Argon ($2/$10
until 2026-11-30, then $4/$20). The filing's own risk factors say limits,
prices and model lifecycles change, and that Anthropic has been capacity
constrained before. Measure: run one week of judge and critic questions
through each, compare cost per accepted judgment at our τ, and write the
result into the ledger's cost column.

**6. Put Spark-X2.6-1.7B through the judge rig.**
Why: it is the only model in the roundup that fits the ProArt (1.769 GB in
GGUF Q4_K_M), and the ProArt is the box with no text seat at all. Policy-clean
on the Thor too, where "many small decision models" is exactly the Thor's job.
Two cautions to carry: it fits in VRAM only if it is not resident beside voice
rungs 3 and 4 (5426 MB of 6141 MB are already spoken for), so it runs on the
CPU or in place of a rung; and the card's throughput numbers come from the
author's own llama.cpp fork on an RTX 4090, not from an Ada laptop. Measure:
the same contexts as item 3, scored by Brier and ECE at τ = 0.5 against d1-2B.

**7. Borrow OverSeer's fixed auxiliary cameras for agent-world.**
Why: four bots that do not co-observe need one shared anchor, and OverSeer
gives the cheapest version we have seen — fixing auxiliary cameras in space
makes changes in them map to localised changes in the world, and the δ-VAE
trick keeps six aux views at 37.5% of the two agent streams' token overhead.
This is design, not weights, so it costs nothing but a day. It also lands on
ideas our own coaching list already has: "Decision feed as the show" and "God
view vs. agent view". Measure: give each bot a fixed camera frame set as part
of its observation, and measure whether misread rate per tick falls (the
existing idea's measure) with a logged diff per tick.

**8. Try Groot-19B-A3B as a scoped-B2 local vision model for one night.**
Why: the 19B is Apache-2.0, has an NVFP4 build at 13.5 GB, and its authors
document 256K context on 128 GB unified machines including GB10 systems —
our class. That is the profile of the SceneBloom precedent: swap the cloud VLM
for a local one under a scoped exception. Measure: one night's prop QA with
the local model against the cloud VLM we already use, comparing verdicts and
cost per call. Needs his OK and our own NVFP4 conversion, so it is ranked
below the two items that need no exception.

**9. Try Unlimited OCR 2 on our own document set.**
Why: 6.7 GB, MIT, 131K context, and its flat attention is the point — the
paper reports peak KV falling from 47.00 GB to 1.77 GB, which is what makes a
300-page document cheap. Our research seat and the judge's evidence path both
read documents we did not write. Measure: parse our own corpus (model cards,
filings, papers) and check the numbers we can check by hand — table structure,
reading order, the figures that appear in more than one place.

**10. Put dated triggers on the three rows that will change.**
MUNDO's promised end-of-November release — check the licence first, then the
size, before anything else. Genie 4 — check whether a model id and a benchmark
appear in the Gemini API docs. Gemini 4 Carbon — check the model list again.
Our three "not available" findings are dated negatives; they decay.

**Two changes to our own practice, from the same roundup.**

- **Give the research protocol a deterministic check where one exists.** The
  OpenAI post's three results took three different release decisions, but the
  through-line is that an AI-produced claim is raw data until an independent
  check turns it into understanding. Our docs already say "say what you did
  not verify"; the missing piece is a *checker*, not a caveat — and
  `github.com/openai/math` is a public corpus of AI-produced claims with
  independent Lean checks, which is a ready-made test set for a verify seat.
- **Record release state with its date.** Three of the seventeen rows are
  "nothing released" or "no licence", and all three are dated negatives that
  expire. A row that says "no licence, as of 2026-11-01" is useful in three
  months; a row that says "no licence" is a stale claim.

## 3. Notes per item — the mechanism behind each row

### 3.1 Perception: ears, and a reader for documents

**Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe 30B-A4B.** The architecture is a hybrid: Mamba-2
layers for sequence state, Transformer layers for attention, a 128-expert MoE
with top-6 routing plus one shared expert, and a "Parallel Expert Encoder" that
puts the ASR encoder (≈600M), a diarisation encoder (≈99M) and a CTC timestamp
head beside the language path. The compatibility table on the model card is
worth quoting exactly, because it is the reason this row is an evaluate rather
than a watch: `NVIDIA Blackwell | Jetson Thor | BF16, FP8, NVFP4 | vLLM 0.32.0`.
The authors' own headline numbers, on the
2026-10-12 evaluation: English Open ASR 9-set mean WER **4.71** (open-weight
next best Qwen3-ASR-1.7B 4.94), long-form **10.98** (Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3
13.37), multilingual 7-language **4.62**, multi-speaker mean **cpWER 15.80**,
Preference-ASR 7.56/6.16. The comparison class matters: the authors separate
"best published" (a commercial system at cpWER 16.83) from "best open-weight".
Their stated limits: this is an offline model, so chunking does not make it a
streaming service; its text-only reasoning is *below* its own base language
model; it is more susceptible to prompt injection; word timestamps need the V1
model runner; and the card gives no VRAM figure and no throughput for the
Jetson Thor — which we would have to measure ourselves.

**Unlimited OCR 2.** The idea is that attention over text and vision is
compressed to a fixed set of working references (~512 tokens) rather than the
whole page history, and decoding runs over an unlimited horizon; the paper
reports peak KV falling from 47.00 GB to 1.77 GB and throughput rising from
360 to 1,315 tokens/s at concurrency 16, with the gain at 100-page scale. The
authors' limits: sparse attention "does not preserve global document
information" such as heading hierarchies, training diversity is limited, and
linear attention is future work. It is a document reader, not a handwriting or
arbitrary-scene OCR. Baidu publishes no quant builds, so the ProArt would need
someone else's 4-bit conversion — which is a second dependency on an unapproved
artefact, and part of why it ranks below the two items above it.

**Hy-SimulMT.** A single speech LLM doing simultaneous zh↔en translation with
its own audio tower (24 layers, 16 kHz, 30 s), and the streaming design is the
interesting part: it refreshes its hypotheses every 1.6 s, and its own
description is that it "starts speaking first and makes corrections
afterwards". The authors report FLEURS 90.3, CoVoST2 75.5, ACL6060 81.2,
RealSI-Sentence 84.7, StreamLAAL ≈3 s, and up to 6.5× faster than Transformers
on an H200 at batch 1. The limits are decisive for us: it is zh↔en only, it has
no language-detection tokens (the caller must declare the language), it is
**not evaluated as ASR at all** — there is no WER anywhere in the paper — and
llama.cpp support exists only through an unmerged pull request.

### 3.2 World models and consolidation: the night's imagination gate

**The gate we would need.** dsmc's own conclusion is that "skills verified in
imagination before admission: the immediate version is a replay gate against
logged decisions (the shadow conductor), not a learned world model". Neither
of this roundup's world models changes that today, and the reasons differ:

- **MUNDO** rehearses a *scene*, not a policy. Its actions are lines in a scene
  program, and a simulator computes the consequences. That is a good way to
  rehearse a robot trajectory or a set-piece, and it cannot rehearse "what
  should the coach say when the builder is stuck", because nothing in it
  chooses. It also has no licence and no weights, and its renderer is a 33B
  video model.
- **Genie 4** is the right shape — action-conditioned, interactive, with audio
  — and it is a waitlisted research preview with no weights, no API id and no
  published numbers. We could not evaluate it if we wanted to.
- **OverSeer** is the most useful *idea* of the three, and it is the cheapest:
  fixed auxiliary cameras as a proxy for a world-centric representation, with a
  δ-VAE so that six redundant views cost 37.5% of the two agent streams' token
  overhead. Their own ablation shows why it works — without the auxiliary views
  the Minecraft numbers collapse to 25.0/16.7/20.8 and Doom's second metric to
  0.0. The training (64 GB300s) is out of reach; the design is not.

**SLEEP, and the sleep cycle with offline gossip.** The brief asks whether
SLEEP is the mechanism behind dsmc's "sleep cycle with offline gossip" and our
night as consolidation. The honest answer has two halves:

- **It is the mechanism behind consolidation, in the parameter sense.** SLEEP's
  "sleep" distils short-term fragile memory into stable long-term parameters:
  parameter expansion unlocks low-rank experts in lower-frequency blocks,
  knowledge seeding distils a smaller self into that larger capacity by
  on-policy distillation plus RL-based imitation learning with a Levenshtein
  reward, and synaptic pruning resets the high-frequency temporary parameters.
  That is consolidation as a training event, at LoRA scale (rank 64, 100 steps
  on 1B–8B backbones). Our d1-2B decision model cannot be expanded that way
  inside our rules, so this half is **not ours to copy** — but the *shape*
  (a wake phase that accumulates and a sleep phase that consolidates, with the
  sleep phase running unattended) is exactly our night.
- **It is not the mechanism behind offline gossip.** SLEEP is intra-model:
  one model distilling its own short-term memory. dsmc's gossip is
  inter-agent: seats exchanging state while nobody waits. The two are
  complementary rather than the same thing, and the gossip half of dsmc's
  design is still unaccounted for by anything in this roundup. That is worth
  saying out loud rather than rounding "sleep" up to a match.
- **The part we can borrow is the dreaming stage**, and it is the part the
  paper's own ablation says matters most: removing dreaming collapses the
  SQuAD results from 48.1/44.3 to 35.7/36.2, and with four levels of continual
  learning to 48.9/46.2. Dreaming is: select experts at random, generate a
  curriculum of synthetic situations, rank them by a gradient-based selection
  criterion, and rehearse only the top. Our analogue is concrete — during the
  night, generate candidate behaviours for the coach or the judge, score them
  with a criterion we already trust (the judge's calibrated score, or the
  measured Brier), and keep only the candidates that improve the next day's
  decisions. One night with and one without is a measurable A/B.
- One more number worth holding: to reach the same accuracy, SFT needs
  4.3× / 3.6× / 4.8× the wall-clock that sleep does. Whatever we copy, the
  rehearsal step is cheaper than the alternative, and the paper discloses no
  GPU hours, so we still cannot price it.

### 3.3 The five open-weight models

They separate cleanly by what they would be *for*:

- **Nanbeige4.3-33B** and **Apex 35B** are general-purpose text models. We
  pay per call for that. Apex's card wants ≥141 GB for its full 262K context,
  its distinguishing benchmarks are CFD and robot simulation, and its "vision"
  demonstrations come from a GPT-6.1-Sol-backed MCP tool rather than from the
  model — it is a text model that borrows eyes.
- **Groot** is the multimodal one, and its 19B is the only artefact in the
  roundup whose authors document operation on a 128 GB unified-memory machine,
  which is our box's shape. The 65B is under qwen-community-1.0, which is
  permissive with two teeth: attribution if the commercial product has more
  than 100M monthly active users or more than US$20M monthly revenue, and a
  **separate licence from Qwen** if we run a Model-as-a-Service or AI Work
  Assistant business for third parties (internal use is exempt). We are
  internal today; that would change if we ever hosted a Groot derivative for
  anyone else.
- **Clover** is a block-diffusion model, and its decision-model variant is the
  row that matters for us. The comparison in their post is in Chinese and
  reads: in an internal validation of 500 groups and 2420 linked questions,
  joint mode answered 95.0% of the questions correctly and got 80.4% of the
  groups fully right; the same model answering independently scored 89.8% and
  62.4%; and **TypeSafe's Jev** scored 63.6% and 15.8% on the same questions.
  Our judge already answers linked questions from a shared state, so this is
  the first published number against our own design — with the awkward
  property that it is a number we cannot reproduce, because the decision
  weights are not published.
- **Spark-X2.6-1.7B** is the only one that fits the ProArt, and it is the
  only one that is policy-clean everywhere: 1.7B dense, 1M context, 1.77 GB in
  GGUF Q4_K_M, Apache-2.0, and upstream llama.cpp now carries the `spark2_5`
  architecture (current build b12032, above the card's b10828 requirement).

### 3.4 Cloud seats and the filing

**Terra** is the only frontier model here that is live, and the numbers are
good for a seat: $1 input / $5 output per 1M tokens, cached input $0.05,
cache writes $1.25 (30-minute) and $2.50 (12-hour), Batch and Flex at 50%
lower, 1,050,000 context, 128,000 max output, effort `none | low | medium |
high | xhigh | max`, function calling and structured outputs supported, image
in and text out, and tool calling that requires the Responses API. Two pricing
details matter for an overnight run: prompts above 272K input tokens are
priced at 2× the input and cache rates and 1.5× the output **for the whole
request**, and Fast mode is 2× Standard. The counterweight is in the system
card: among reasoning traces of 750–1,250 tokens, Terra follows the requested
chain-of-thought instruction in **14.8%** of cases, against 23.2% for GPT-6
Sol and 44.8% for GPT-6.1 Sol. A seat that asks for a particular way of
thinking and then checks it got that way is exactly our critic's shape, so
this number is not a footnote for us.

**Carbon** is a dated negative: no model id, not in the Gemini API model list
or pricing page on 2026-11-01, and access through a Fairwind Program cohort for
vetted cyber defenders. The live sibling is Argon, at $2/$10 input/output
through 2026-11-30 and $4/$20 from 2026-12-01, with implicit caching only
($0.10, then $0.20) and Batch at $1/$5.

**The Anthropic S-1** — the brief asks for it only if it bears on our tools,
and the answer is: a narrow slice does, and the rest does not. What bears on
us, in the filing's own words: "We have also changed, and may in the future
change, usage limits, rate limits, or model and product capabilities"; the
pricing structures change from time to time; deprecation of older models may
be limited or delayed; and on capacity — "In the past, we have significantly
underestimated our compute requirements, which left us capacity constrained."
Those are our seats' failure modes: a 429 or an overload is a night that does
not produce a report. What does not bear on us: the $16,286M of H1 revenue,
the $8,945M of compute, and the $141.1B of capital raised — and the compute
commitments themselves (Google ≥$114.5B, Amazon ≥$110.0B, Microsoft ≥$44.9B,
SpaceXAI up to $120.5B, plus Broadcom leases and smaller arrangements) are
frontier-scale facts, not our costs. Two cautions for our own notes: the
aggregate committed-spend figure is **blank in the filing**, so the totals
circulating in the press are reporters' sums and must never be quoted as
Anthropic's number; and the headline $80.7B H1 net loss is mostly non-cash
fair-value changes on convertible notes, derivatives and SAFEs ($10.9B and
$72.4B), not operating burn — operating income was $974M in Q2. The one
number we should act on is the cheap one: Fable 5.1 cut the cost of typical
workloads by ≈25% (up to 45% for complex coding and highly agentic tasks) and
Opus 5.5 cut cost per task by ≈40%. Our Claude subscription is one of our
seats' providers, so a re-price is the action.

### 3.5 The practice worth adopting

The OpenAI post and its artefact are the most reusable thing in the roundup,
and not because of the mathematics. Three results, three different release
decisions: the math work was published openly; the complexity result was
published as a natural-language proof plus a Lean formalisation and explained
with external academic collaborators; the physics result was deferred by its
own external collaborators. The stated frame is that the manuscripts are "a
form of raw research data, rather than polished academic papers", with credit
deferred to the community, and `github.com/openai/math` makes the working
visible: 721 manuscripts in 373 families, roughly 42% of top-line results
formalized in Lean, abridged reasoning traces for ten families, and a
formalization catalogue with verification configurations.

That is our own practice, already: our docs never claim, they say what was not
verified, and the judge refuses verdicts rather than making them. Two things
this roundup adds. First, a deterministic check where one exists — Lean there,
a rule engine or a checker here — rather than only a second opinion, because
the repository's 42% is a reminder that most of the corpus is *not* formally
checked, and a second opinion is not a checker. Second, a public test set: the
repository is AI-produced claims paired with independent checks, which is
precisely the shape of a verify seat's input, and it is free.

## 4. What we did not verify

Stated plainly, per our rule that untested paths are labelled.

- **GPT-6.1 Terra's announcement benchmarks** — "Agents' Last Exam +15 pp at
  ~1/3 cost", "~9× lower cost per task than Claude Opus 5.5 at max effort",
  "Terminal-Bench 4.0 50.3% at max". They are not in the model documentation
  or the system card, and openai.com returns 403 to scripted fetches, so they
  come from OpenAI's announcement via coverage. **Unverified here.** The pricing,
  context, effort dial, endpoints and the CoT controllability figure in the row
  above were read from OpenAI's own documentation and system card.
- **MUNDO's speed and scale** — 1.5 fps on eight A800s, ~40k training pairs,
  10–20 minutes per scene reconstruction, the MiniMax-H3 renderer, "no
  long-horizon memory mechanism yet", small objects floating, hallucination.
  The project page does not carry these; they are in the authors' Hugging Face
  blog, read through search. The verdict does not rest on them.
- **Unlimited OCR 2's paper tables** — MPDocBench 84.59 overall,
  MarathonDocBench 77.06 for 100–300 pages, peak KV 47.00 GB → 1.77 GB,
  360 → 1,315 tokens/s at concurrency 16, and the 3B total / ~500M active split.
  These come from the v2 PDF in the repository via a delegated read. What we
  verified directly: the MIT licences, the 6.688 GB weight size, the
  Sparse Reference Sliding Window Attention mechanism, and the MarathonDocBench
  release.
- **OverSeer's** wall-clock training cost, and whether the released datasets
  carry the camera geometry we would need.
- **Groot's** base-model attributions for the 65B, 238B and 280B (stated in
  the card text; the API's `base_model` field was empty), and whether
  `spark2_5` performs well on Ada sm_89 — the card's throughput numbers come
  from the author's own llama.cpp fork on an RTX 4090 and an M5 Pro.
- **Nemotron on our box**: no Thor VRAM figure and no Thor throughput in
  NVIDIA's documentation. "This is an offline model" is the authors' wording.
- **Carbon and Genie 4**: the "no model id" findings are dated 2026-11-01 and
  decay. Re-check before acting on them.
- **The Fable 5.1 figures** (≈25% / up to 45%) could not be cross-checked
  outside the filing; Anthropic's own what's-new page renders without
  extractable text.
- **Terra-specific compaction**: `context_management` and `previous_response_id`
